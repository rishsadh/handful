Choosing what to eat or prepare is a common part of everyday life. However, the decision can become more difficult when a person has to search through a very large number of options. Digital food platforms provide greater variety, but they also require users to compare many recipes before finding something suitable. This creates a practical need for a system that can reduce a large catalogue into a smaller and more relevant set of choices.

Method detail

Digital food platforms give you everything. Choice overload is the idea that too many options make choosing harder — it is why this project exists, and it is not something this project measured.

A shortlist that ignores the dividers tends to collapse behind one of them: six fifteen-minute pastas, or six all-day braises. No price, no calories, no cuisine, no "diversity score" — each would need data the box doesn't hold, or would break the property in the programme that keeps the answers whole.

The four bands, 15 / 30 / 60 minutes, and the cap of three are fixed by the study's protocol and were not tuned to the result.

Method detail

Maximise match score: exactly six recipes, at most three per band.

That is the whole linear programme. Each x may sit anywhere between 0 and 1, and every one still comes out a whole card.

Method detail

The match score

A frozen text model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) turns each card's name and ingredients into numbers. A person's taste is the average of the cards they rated 4 or 5 before the cutoff. A card's match score — pencilled in its corner — is how close it sits to that taste, on a 0–1 scale. It says "reads like things you liked." It does not say "you will enjoy this."

Why the answers stay whole

The dividers are disjoint, so the constraint matrix is totally unimodular and every vertex of the feasible region is integral. The programme never has to be told x is 0 or 1; the geometry makes it so. Add a price or a calorie constraint and that property breaks, which is why they are not here.

Feasibility first

The optimum lies at a vertex.

Drag the point. Every split between the two recipes scores less than the better whole one.

Method detail

You cannot draw 229,000 variables. So hold five of the six cards fixed and let two compete for the last slot: the weakest pick, A, and the best card that could take its place, B. The rule xA + xB = 1 is a straight line inside the unit square; a linear objective is best at one end of it. A whole card, never a split.

It is the same optimum the solver finds on the full problem, not a different one — the slice is a two-variable view of it, which is what the graphical method in the syllabus is for.

Optimality is certified by strong duality.

The programme that maximises match and its twin that minimises a price on each rule meet at the same value. A gap of zero is the certificate.

Method detail

Every linear programme has a dual that runs the other way. At the optimum the two are equal; strong duality. The dual also prices the dividers: β for a divider is how much match one extra slot behind it would buy. Not full: zero, the rule cost nothing. Full: the exact cost of variety, in match-score units. Not money, not effort, not psychology.

A popularity baseline outperformed the personalised method.

Validation test on 812 real people against ratings they had not yet made. The bar sits clear of "no difference": the gap is real, and it is not in our favour. The final run, on 530 people and 2018 ratings, came out the same way.

Choice overload motivates the study; it is not a finding.

The idea that a smaller, well-spread set is easier to choose from is why shortlisting is worth studying. It is not something this project observed. No participant was recruited, no survey was run, no decision was timed for the record.

Method detail

It measures

  • Whether a shortlist retrieves recipes the person later rated highly
  • Ranking quality, and how far the shortlist spreads across dividers
  • Whether a valid shortlist exists at all, and how often it does not
  • How the answer moves when the shortlist length and the cap change

It does not measure

  • Choice overload, stress, regret or decision difficulty
  • Confidence, satisfaction, or how quickly anyone decides
  • Nutrition, diet quality, allergy safety or any health outcome
  • Anything about people in general, or about a live deployment
